# Building for First-Time Users: Founders Talk Tech, Trust and AI at IndabaX South Sudan 2026

*Poni Henry · 5 October 2026*

South Sudan's tech founders are building for a market where most customers have never used an app before. That was the common thread of the founders panel at IndabaX South Sudan 2026, organised by Scenius Hub in Juba.

Moderated by Gaso Grace Pascal, the panel brought together three founders at different stages:

- **Nelson Kwaje**, founder of Scenius Hub and CEO of DRF, with more than ten years in the industry.

- **John Marit**, founder of Mobilaws, a platform that makes South Sudan's laws easier to find and understand.

- **Johnson Achuk**, founder of AcademiaPro, a school management system now used in five schools.

Over roughly 35 minutes, they discussed the shape of the local market, how they started, how they earned trust, what they have learned, how they use AI, and what support startups need most.

## The market: small, first-time and hardware-hungry

Nelson Kwaje named three reasons tech products struggle in South Sudan: market size, policy and infrastructure, and inexperience.

**Market size.** He compared it to a seasonal river: "when the water level is low, nothing can flow." Apps that earn small platform fees, often 0.1% to 3% per transaction, need volume. Without about 5,000 users, he argued, a fee-based app will struggle to earn a margin.

**Policy and infrastructure.** Whole product categories depend on government decisions. He pointed to the US opening up precise location data, which made Uber and every location-based app possible. In South Sudan, missing policy decisions, especially in financial services, block products before they start.

The record backs this up. The US government switched off GPS Selective Availability on 1 May 2000, making civilian GPS about ten times more accurate overnight, and the Department of Defense later credited that accuracy with spurring new products ([GPS.gov](https://gps.gov/selective-availability); [DoD report to Congress, 2002](https://www.gps.gov/congress/reports/2002/biennial-gps-report.pdf)).

**Inexperience.** Kwaje was careful to say this is not an insult. Many founders carry "untested knowledge" from YouTube and courses that has not met a real market. The result is plenty of launches and many quiet failures. "We do launches but we don't do funerals," he said, suggesting that openly marking failed products would make founders more careful.

### Who the users are

Most users in South Sudan are first-time digital users, Kwaje said. People buying airtime, using MoMo or mobile banking have usually not switched from another app, unlike in markets such as the US. That means founders must budget for user education. He noted that earlier mobile money services trained many of the users who later moved to MoMo.

The data support this picture. South Sudan had about 1.61 million internet users in late 2025, 13.2% of the population, leaving roughly 87% offline ([DataReportal, Digital 2026](https://datareportal.com/reports/digital-2026-south-sudan)). Mobile money only arrived in 2019 with m-Gurush and Nilepay, and fewer than 1% of adults held a mobile money account in 2021 ([GSMA](https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/connectivity-for-good/mobile-for-development/blog/south-sudan-mobile-moneys-role-in-the-road-to-recovery/)).

The market is also hardware-heavy. Smartphone and laptop ownership is low, and on campuses the need is often a device, not software. As device ownership rises, he said, every product built on top of it will benefit.

Mobile connections equalled only about 48% of the population in late 2025, and some of those carry voice and SMS without internet ([DataReportal, Digital 2026](https://datareportal.com/reports/digital-2026-south-sudan)). GSMA also lists low phone penetration and limited electricity among the barriers to mobile money uptake ([GSMA](https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/connectivity-for-good/mobile-for-development/blog/south-sudan-mobile-moneys-role-in-the-road-to-recovery/)).

## Origin stories: law and classrooms

**Mobilaws.** John Marit started Mobilaws because legal information in South Sudan is hard to reach. Laws sit as long documents on sites like the Ministry of Justice website. Mobilaws simplifies that: a user looking for a specific provision can ask for it and go straight to it, rather than scrolling through whole documents. Without access to the law, he said, people cannot tell where they have gone right or wrong.

**AcademiaPro.** Johnson Achuk studied in Kampala before returning home. In South Sudan he saw teachers spending more time on paperwork than on students, unlike the close teacher–student relationships he had known in high school. AcademiaPro aims to take administrative work off teachers so students can stay at the centre.

## Trust and adoption

Both founders said trust comes before any feature.

For Marit, trust in a legal product starts with awareness. Every citizen has a right to know the constitution and the rights that protect them. Many offences, he argued, happen because people do not know the law or where to turn for help. Mobilaws positions itself first as an educational tool.

Achuk was frank: winning schools was "very, very difficult." Schools will not adopt a product from a team they do not trust, however good it is. AcademiaPro's approach had four parts:

1.  Visit many schools and treat every rejection as feedback.

2.  Act on that feedback, including formally registering the company.

3.  Start with schools where the team already had ties, led by alumni on the team.

4.  Offer free trials so schools could test the product without paying first.

Momentum followed the first sign-up. AcademiaPro is now used in five schools. Legacy Global Academy was first, and a large school joined later after the team iterated and built mutual trust.

## Challenges and lessons

The two younger founders named the same obstacle: low trust in, and familiarity with, digital tools.

Marit said most government institutions still work manually. The officials who run them are often the same people he hopes will use Mobilaws, so it is hard to convince them a digital product works when their own workplace does not use one. If government went digital first, he suggested, workers would already understand digital products, making private-sector adoption easier.

International rankings reflect the gap he describes: South Sudan placed 192nd of 193 countries in the UN's 2024 E-Government Development Index ([UN DESA](https://publicadministration.un.org/egovkb/en-us/Data/Country-Information/id/193-South-Sudan)).

Achuk agreed that digital literacy is the main barrier. Selling to a school means first explaining how the system improves its workflow, then sensitising staff on why the change matters.

GSMA reaches a similar conclusion about mobile money, noting that low literacy and digital literacy limit uptake, especially among displaced communities ([GSMA](https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/connectivity-for-good/mobile-for-development/blog/south-sudan-mobile-moneys-role-in-the-road-to-recovery/)).

His biggest lesson was to move slowly. With little competition in South Sudan, founders are not forced to rush features or chase as many customers as possible. He learned to take time, iterate, and then go to market.

## AI in practice

All three founders already use AI, and their examples were concrete rather than hype.

**Automating verification (Marit).** A client was receiving more than 15,000 invoices and documents a month, all checked by hand and stored in a secured room. Marit's team built an AI-assisted workflow:

1.  Clients upload documents remotely instead of visiting the office.

2.  AI checks each document is complete and legitimate.

3.  AI checks whether the document has been used before.

4.  A staff member reviews the AI's work.

5.  AI matches submitted documents against the client's records.

No one lost their job, he stressed; staff now monitor the process instead. Reviewers can work from Nairobi, Kampala or South Africa. "AI is not just a chatbot you can prompt," he said. He also runs a cloud-based AI agent for development, research and drafting.

**Shortening the gap between idea and output (Kwaje).** Kwaje uses AI for skills he needs only occasionally. In one client job, an AI assistant in Excel cleaned 17,000 entries, stripping letter prefixes and suffixes, converting values to integers and handling duplicates. Doing it with macros would have taken him two to three hours. He also uses AI for small coding tasks and for documents, keeping separate workspaces that remember company details, logos and his letter format. He urged the audience to pay for AI, calling the gap between free and paid tools huge: around \$20 a month, he said, buys what feels like a consultant normally paid \$3,000.

**Learning faster (Achuk).** Achuk uses AI to level up as a developer. AcademiaPro does not use AI yet, but the team is building a feature to analyse attendance and results. It will flag students whose performance is dropping compared to last term and recommend how they can improve.

## The support startups need

Asked what support would make the biggest difference, the panel pointed first to the economy. An unstable currency and inflation make it hard for founders to price and sell their products. Greater market stability would let them plan with confidence.

World Bank figures show the scale of the problem. Average inflation, measured through food prices, reached 234% in FY25, while the official exchange rate depreciated by 198% ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/southsudan)). Businesses have adapted: m-Gurush, for example, offers US-dollar-only accounts to shield users from currency swings ([GSMA](https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/connectivity-for-good/mobile-for-development/blog/south-sudan-mobile-moneys-role-in-the-road-to-recovery/)).

The second need is financing: real access to loans, seed funding and angel investors.

UNDP's South Sudan Accelerator Lab names the same pair of problems, lack of capital and unstable markets, as compounding challenges for entrepreneurs, and reports that aspiring founders often say they do not know how to access finance ([UNDP](https://www.undp.org/south-sudan/tracking-impact-financial-literacy-disadvantaged-entrepreneurs-south-sudan)).

## What it means for founders

The panel's message to aspiring founders in the IndabaX audience was practical:

- **Budget for education.** Your users are likely first-time digital users, so teaching them is part of the product.

- **Earn trust before you sell.** Register properly, start with your own network, and lower the risk with free trials.

- **Test your knowledge early.** Untested ideas behave unpredictably; real users are the test.

- **Move deliberately.** Low competition gives room to iterate before scaling.

- **Use AI seriously.** Treat it as a working tool for automation, coding and documents, not just a chatbot.

Gaso closed the segments by urging the audience to look for problems in their own communities and to connect with the founders on stage as future collaborators and co-founders.
