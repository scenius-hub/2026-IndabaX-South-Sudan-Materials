# AI in Banking: Gains, Risks and Access to Finance in South Sudan

*Poni Henry · 5 October 2026*

Artificial intelligence is already deciding who gets a loan, which transaction looks suspicious and how quickly a customer gets an answer. On Thursday, 1 October 2026, Stanbic Bank South Sudan brought that conversation to IndabaX South Sudan 2026 at Scenius Hub, Juba, with a 30-minute lightning talk on what AI has done for banking, what it has done to it, and what it could mean for access to finance at home.

The session, held from 9:40 to 10:10 AM, spoke to a room of students, developers, researchers, innovators and industry professionals. It opened with Stanbic's own digital journey before moving through the gains, the risks and the local opportunity, and closed with questions from the audience.

## The gains: faster, safer, smarter banking

AI's clearest wins in banking are speed and protection. Tasks that once took days of paperwork can now be handled in minutes, and systems watch millions of transactions for signs of fraud far faster than any human team could.

- **Faster services:** automated checks speed up account opening, payments and loan processing.

- **Fraud detection:** models learn what normal activity looks like and flag what doesn't, often before money is lost.

- **Better credit decisions:** lenders can look at a wider range of data to judge whether a borrower can repay.

- **Chatbots and mobile banking:** customers get answers at any hour, on the phone in their pocket, without visiting a branch.

## The risks: when the machine gets it wrong

The same tools that speed up banking can also cause real harm, and the talk did not shy away from that. An AI system is only as fair as the data it learns from, and a wrong decision made at scale affects thousands of people at once.

- **Biased lending:** models trained on past decisions can repeat old patterns that shut out certain groups.

- **Privacy concerns:** AI depends on large amounts of personal and financial data that must be protected.

- **AI-enabled fraud and scams:** criminals use the same technology to fake voices, documents and messages.

- **Changing jobs:** some roles shrink as tasks are automated, while new ones appear.

Banks manage these risks through human oversight of key decisions, regular testing of models for bias, strong data protection, and educating customers about new kinds of scams.

## Governance first: guarding the data

The speaker returned again and again to one point: AI in finance must be governed. Clear rules on how AI is used, who is accountable and how decisions are checked are not optional extras, but the foundation that makes the technology safe to use.

A big part of that is data. The information banks handle, from account details to transaction records, is highly sensitive, and it should never be fed into AI tools casually. The speaker stressed that staff must not share this data with AI tools without proper safeguards.

To prevent that, banks put mechanisms and partnerships in place that control which AI tools staff can use and what data can go into them. The lesson for everyone in the room was simple: think before you paste.

## What AI could solve in South Sudan

For South Sudan, the biggest promise of AI is reaching people the formal financial system has left out. Many people have no bank account, no credit history and few branches within reach, and most of the economy still runs on cash.

- **Limited access to banking:** mobile tools and chatbots can serve customers far from a branch.

- **No credit history:** alternative data, such as mobile money activity or regular payments, could help lenders judge people who have never borrowed before.

- **Cash dependence:** safer, simpler digital payments can build trust in moving away from cash.

- **Rural communities and farmers:** AI could help tailor loans and services to seasonal incomes and remote locations.

The message was that technology alone is not enough. These tools only work if they are designed for local conditions, protect customer data and earn people's trust.

## Where young talent fits in

Building these solutions will take local people who understand both the technology and the context. The financial sector needs data scientists, software developers, cybersecurity specialists and people who can explain AI decisions in plain language to customers and regulators.

For students and developers, the path starts with practical skills in data, programming and security, and with community spaces like IndabaX and Scenius Hub where they can learn, build and connect with industry.

## The takeaway

Attendees left with a balanced picture: AI can make banking faster, safer and more inclusive, but only if it is well governed and the data behind it is protected. For South Sudan, the real prize is using it to bring more people into the financial system, and the young people in the room are the ones best placed to build it.
